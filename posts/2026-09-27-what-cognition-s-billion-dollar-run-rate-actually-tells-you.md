Cognition, the company behind the AI coding agent Devin, closed a $2 billion Series E on September 8 at a $48 billion valuation, led by Andreessen Horowitz and Accel. Bloomberg reported this week that the company's annualized revenue run rate is tracking toward $1 billion this month, up from $492 million in May. Cognition itself said in September that the run rate had topped $900 million. Either way, that is roughly a doubling in about four months, for a company founded in November 2023.

The customer list is the part that makes this more than a funding headline. Nvidia, Citigroup, Mercedes-Benz, Goldman Sachs, NASA, and the US Army and Navy are named users. Those are not organizations that adopt unproven developer tools casually. Something real is happening in enterprise appetite for agentic coding tools.

## Read the number the way you'd read a metric in your own system

Here is the detail worth sitting with, because it's a useful habit regardless of what company you're looking at. Cognition's own confirmed figure is a run rate that topped $900 million in September. The $1 billion figure is a projection, from an anonymous source, based on how this specific month is trending. A run rate is one month's revenue multiplied by twelve. It is a standard, ordinary metric to report, and there's nothing dishonest about using it. But it describes a pace, not a closed year of recognized revenue, and a strong month can make that pace look bigger than the underlying trend actually is.

This is the same discipline you'd want applied to a dashboard in your own product. A metric that's technically accurate can still create a more confident picture than the underlying data supports, depending on which window you report it over. The gap between "our run rate topped $900 million" and headlines reading "hits $1 billion in annualized revenue" is small in absolute terms and worth noticing anyway, because it's exactly the kind of rounding that compounds when nobody checks it.

## What Devin is actually being used for

Cognition has been fairly consistent that Devin isn't sold as a replacement for engineers. In earlier reporting, CEO Scott Wu described its role as absorbing the long tail of work engineers tend to avoid: bringing old software up to date, migrating applications from one platform to another, the unglamorous maintenance that accumulates in any codebase old enough to matter. That's a meaningfully different pitch than "an AI that writes your product for you," and it's probably a more honest one. Legacy modernization and platform migration are bounded, well specified problems with a clear definition of done, which is exactly the kind of task current agentic tools tend to handle well. Open ended feature design with ambiguous requirements is a different problem, and the customer testimonials so far are concentrated in the former.

## Why the growth is still worth taking seriously

None of the above is an argument that the number is fake or that the growth isn't real. Enterprise sales cycles at companies like Goldman Sachs and the US Navy do not move on hype alone. Procurement, security review, and integration into existing engineering workflows take months, and organizations like that don't typically expand usage of a tool that isn't delivering measured value. The honest read is that both things are true at once: real, substantial enterprise demand for agentic coding tools handling scoped, well defined work, reported through a revenue metric that, like every run rate, describes momentum more precisely than it describes a guaranteed trajectory.

## What I take from this as someone building with these tools

The part worth carrying forward isn't a verdict on Cognition specifically. It's a template for evaluating any fast growing AI infrastructure claim, including the tools you're deciding whether to adopt yourself.

1. Separate the metric from the framing around it. A run rate, an ARR figure, a valuation, each describes something narrower than it sounds like in a headline. Knowing exactly what was measured, and over what window, tells you more than the number alone.

2. Look at what the tool is actually scoped to do, not what the marketing implies it does. Devin's traction concentrates in bounded modernization and migration work, not open ended engineering. That's a real, valuable niche. It's also a narrower claim than "AI that codes for you," and the narrower claim is the one that's actually earning enterprise trust right now.

3. Enterprise adoption at this speed, at these companies, is still a meaningful signal, even accounting for all of the above. Skepticism about how a number is reported isn't the same as skepticism about whether the underlying demand is real.

Agentic coding tools are clearly past the novelty stage. The more useful question, for a business evaluating one or an engineer deciding how much to trust one, isn't whether the growth is impressive. It's exactly what job the tool is good at right now, and whether that job matches the one you actually need done.
